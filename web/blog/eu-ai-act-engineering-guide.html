EU AI Act: What Engineering Teams Need to Know

A practical guide to Article 12 traceability, risk classifications, and the code-level changes your AI systems need before enforcement deadlines hit.

By Alyshia Ledlie December 15, 2024 12 min read

You shipped a model to production last Tuesday. It processes customer data from 14 EU member states. Can you tell a regulator exactly what data flowed through that model, what decisions it made, and why? If the answer is "not yet," this guide is for you.

The EU AI Act is the first comprehensive AI regulation with real enforcement teeth. For engineering teams, this isn't a policy memo to forward to legal—it's a set of technical requirements that affect how you build, instrument, and deploy AI systems. The good news: most of these requirements align with practices you should be doing anyway. The bad news: "should be" and "are" rarely overlap.

Risk Classification: Where Does Your System Land?

Before you write a single line of compliance code, you need to know which risk tier your AI system falls into. This determines everything—from whether you need basic transparency labels or a full conformity assessment with third-party audits.

Risk Tier Examples Engineering Impact
High Risk Credit scoring, hiring tools, medical diagnostics, critical infrastructure Full audit trail, conformity assessment, continuous monitoring, human oversight hooks
Limited Risk Chatbots, content generation, recommendation systems Transparency labels, user notification that AI is in use
Minimal Risk Spam filters, inventory optimization, code completion No specific requirements (voluntary best practices encouraged)
Watch Out

General-purpose AI models (including most LLMs) have their own transparency and documentation requirements under Title V of the Act, regardless of downstream risk classification. If you're deploying GPT-4, Claude, or similar models, you need to document model capabilities, limitations, and intended uses even if your application is "minimal risk."

Classification isn't always obvious. A customer support chatbot is "limited risk" until it starts making decisions about refunds or account closures—then it may cross into "high risk." Map your system's actual decision authority, not just its label.

Article 12: The Traceability Requirement

Article 12 is where the rubber meets the road for engineering teams. It requires that high-risk AI systems have "automatic recording of events" (logging) throughout the system's lifecycle. In practice, this means:

What Article 12 Requires

  • Input logging — Record every prompt, query, or data input the system processes
  • Output logging — Capture every response, decision, or action the system produces
  • Operational context — Model version, configuration, feature flags, and environment state at decision time
  • Temporal traceability — Timestamps that allow reconstruction of the decision sequence
  • Performance monitoring — Ongoing measurement of accuracy, fairness, and robustness metrics

The key phrase is "reconstruction." A regulator should be able to take any output your system produced and trace backwards to understand exactly what inputs, context, and model state produced it. If your current logging amounts to console.log('got response'), you have work to do.

What This Looks Like in OpenTelemetry

OpenTelemetry is the natural fit for Article 12 compliance. Its trace/span model maps directly to the "automatic recording of events" requirement, and its vendor-neutral design means you're not locked into a single observability provider.

The critical detail: you must log enough context to reconstruct the decision, but not so much that you violate GDPR. This tension between Article 12 (log everything) and GDPR (minimize data) is the central engineering challenge. More on that in the data governance section.

Instrumentation Patterns for Compliance

Bolt-on compliance doesn't work. Teams that try to add Article 12 logging after the fact end up with incomplete traces, missing context, and audit gaps. Instead, build compliance into your instrumentation layer from the start.

Pattern 1: Decision Boundary Tracing

Instrument every point where your AI system makes or influences a decision. Not just the model call—the full decision boundary including pre-processing, guardrails, and post-processing.

Pattern 2: Compliance Metadata as Span Attributes

Standardize a set of compliance-specific attributes across all AI spans. This makes audit queries straightforward and ensures consistency across teams.

Pattern 3: Audit-Ready Log Retention

The AI Act doesn't specify exact retention periods, but enforcement actions require historical evidence. Design your storage layer for long-term retention with efficient querying.

  • Hot storage (0–30 days) — Full trace data, queryable in real time
  • Warm storage (30–365 days) — Compressed traces, available within minutes
  • Cold storage (1–5 years) — Archived for regulatory requests, retrievable within hours
Practical Tip

Use OpenTelemetry Collector's export pipeline to fan out traces to both your real-time observability tool (hot) and a long-term archive (cold). This separates operational needs from compliance needs without duplicating instrumentation code.

Data Governance and PII

Here's the tension every engineering team faces: Article 12 says "log the inputs and outputs." GDPR says "minimize personal data processing." When your LLM processes a customer's name, address, and financial history to make a decision, you need to log enough for traceability without creating a PII liability.

Strategies That Work

  • PII detection at the instrumentation layer — Scan inputs and outputs before logging. Flag spans that contain PII, and apply redaction or tokenization before the data hits your trace storage.
  • Separate PII from trace data — Store a reference ID in the trace; store the actual PII in a GDPR-compliant vault with its own access controls and retention policies.
  • Consent-aware logging levels — Not all users have the same data processing consent. Your instrumentation should respect consent tiers—full logging for users who've consented, redacted logging otherwise.
  • Right-to-deletion support — GDPR's right to erasure applies to trace data too. Design your storage so PII can be deleted without destroying the structural integrity of the trace.
Common Mistake

Don't dump raw prompts containing customer data into your trace store. A prompt like "Process refund for John Smith, account #12345, $500 charge on 12/01" contains at least three categories of PII. Detect and handle it at the instrumentation boundary, not after it's been stored across three observability systems.

Testing for Compliance

Compliance isn't something you verify once and forget. The AI Act requires ongoing monitoring and risk management (Articles 9 and 15). Your test suite should reflect this.

What to Test

  • Trace completeness — Given an input, does the resulting trace contain all required compliance attributes? Write integration tests that assert on span attribute presence.
  • PII handling — Feed test inputs containing known PII patterns. Verify that traces contain redacted versions, not raw data.
  • Decision reconstruction — Take a trace ID from your test environment, and verify you can reconstruct the full decision context—inputs, model version, guardrail results, and output.
  • Retention and deletion — Verify that trace data moves through your hot/warm/cold storage tiers on schedule, and that PII deletion propagates correctly.
  • Fairness metrics — Track decision outcomes across demographic groups. The AI Act's Article 10 requires data governance that addresses bias. Automated fairness tests catch drift before regulators do.

Engineering Readiness Checklist

Use this as a starting point for your team's compliance sprint. Not every item applies to every system—start with your risk classification and work down.

High-Risk Systems

  • Risk classification documented — System is formally classified under the AI Act's risk framework with supporting rationale
  • Article 12 logging implemented — All AI decision points produce traces with input, output, model version, and operational context
  • PII detection and handling — Instrumentation layer detects PII and applies redaction/tokenization before storage
  • Human oversight hooks — Systems flag low-confidence decisions for human review; override mechanisms are in place
  • Fairness monitoring — Automated tests track decision outcomes across demographic groups; alerts fire on drift
  • Audit-ready storage — Trace data retention meets regulatory requirements; data is retrievable for inspection
  • Decision reconstruction tested — Given any trace ID, the team can reconstruct the full decision context within 24 hours
  • Incident response plan — Documented process for AI system failures, including notification to relevant authorities

Limited-Risk Systems

  • Transparency labels implemented — Users are informed when interacting with AI-generated content or AI-assisted decisions
  • AI disclosure in user-facing interfaces — Chatbots, content generators, and similar systems clearly identify as AI
  • Basic logging in place — Sufficient logging to investigate issues and respond to user complaints

What's Next

The AI Act's enforcement timeline is staggered, but the technical work to achieve compliance can't be. If your systems fall into the high-risk tier, the instrumentation, storage, and testing infrastructure takes months to build properly—and that's assuming you start with a clean architecture.

Start with classification. Know what tier your systems occupy. Then work through the checklist above, prioritizing Article 12 traceability (it's the foundation everything else builds on) and PII handling (it's where AI Act and GDPR intersect, and where mistakes are most expensive).
